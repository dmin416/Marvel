### Core rules for any scene

- **Legal status is not the same as ability.** Powers, wealth, training, approval from one property owner, and press popularity do not create police powers.
- **Permission is limited.** An owner can generally grant access to property under their control, not the neighboring owners' rooftops or blanket authority over public streets.
- **Stopping harm is different from imposing punishment.** Defense of others and a lawful citizen's arrest may justify limited intervention, but not private punishment.
- **An officer's badge is an appointment, not a certificate.** Training alone does not confer police or peace-officer status.
- **Public recognition is not immunity.** A celebrated hero may still face criminal investigation or civil liability for unjustified injury or damage.
- **Keep time periods separate.** A statute adopted years later does not automatically govern the 1999–2007 story.

## Part I — New York City vigilantes, superheroes, citizen's arrests, and police authority (MAIN REFERENCE)

The following is the **full NYC law document** previously developed for this project, incorporated as the central section of this master. Its topic numbering and content are preserved; headings are adjusted one level to fit the larger document.

### Original NYC legal reference (1999–2007)

> **Reference period:** New York City, approximately **1999–2007**.  
> **Purpose:** Real-world legal background for **D's alternate Marvel continuity**, **not** a declaration that any unplayed event has occurred.  
> **Continuity status:** No special D-world superhero-registration or vigilante-enforcement statute has been established as of the **March 31, 2000** story checkpoint. Marvel-specific laws must be separately adopted if desired.  
> **Historical caution:** This is a practical legal overview for fiction, not a date-certified transcription of every statute or NYPD policy in force in each year. Some linked modern statutory texts contain later amendments; verify the historical version when exact wording or penalties matter.

### 1. Basic legal distinction

**New York does not generally prohibit someone merely for calling themself a vigilante or superhero.** What matters is what that individual actually does: protecting others, making an arrest, injuring someone, trespassing, interfering with police, or destroying property.

A civilian hero can sometimes interrupt crimes, protect people, make a citizen's arrest, and cooperate with police. **Hero status does not confer police powers, prosecution immunity, or a right to punish offenders.**

| Activity | Ordinary civilian / superhero | NYPD officer |
|---|---|---|
| Wear a superhero costume | Generally lawful, subject to applicable restrictions | Department uniform and identification rules apply |
| Patrol public streets | Generally lawful | Authorized as part of police duties |
| Stop an ongoing assault | May use legally justified force | May use legally justified force |
| Arrest a suspect | Limited citizen's-arrest power | Statutory police arrest power |
| Use force during an arrest | Reasonably necessary force, under strict limits | Reasonably necessary force, plus police policies |
| Search a suspect | No general police search power | Searches allowed under defined legal circumstances |
| Investigate | May gather information lawfully | Official investigative powers |
| Enter private property | Requires consent or a recognized legal justification | Requires a warrant or recognized exception, as applicable |
| Question a suspect | Cannot compel answers | Can investigate and question within legal limits |
| Seize weapons or property | No general seizure power; narrow emergency/arrest situations may justify temporary handling | May seize evidence or dangerous items under defined legal rules |
| Use deadly force | Strictly limited | Strictly limited by law and department policy |
| Punish a criminal | Not authorized | Also not authorized outside the judicial process |

**Core real-world statutes:** New York **Penal Law Article 35** (justification and physical force) and **Criminal Procedure Law Article 140** (arrests).

### 2. Citizen's arrests — CPL § 140.30

New York law recognizes arrests made by **private persons** in particular circumstances.

#### Felonies

A civilian may arrest someone for a **felony that the person actually committed**, even if the civilian did not witness the offense firsthand, subject to the law's other requirements. This is a higher-risk power than it might appear: an erroneous accusation can create substantial legal liability. The grounds for using force must also satisfy the applicable justification rules.

*Marvel example:* Spider-Man obtains reliable information about an armed robbery and stops the actual robber. A lawful citizen's arrest may be possible, but the accusation must be right and the restraint must stay within legal limits.

#### Misdemeanors and other lesser offenses

A private person may arrest for an **offense actually committed in that person's presence**, subject to geographic limits and the statute's requirements. For an offense other than a felony, the arrest must take place in the county where the offense was committed.

*Marvel example:* A hero personally witnesses intentional vandalism of a parked car and restrains the offender until police arrive. The facts may support a citizen's arrest; a suspicion based only on a rumor would not automatically do so.

#### Notice of arrest

The civilian ordinarily must inform the person of the reason for the arrest, subject to statutory exceptions when flight, resistance, or the circumstances make notice impractical.

**Key distinction:** A private person is not simply an unofficial police officer. The legal arrest power is narrower and mistakes can have serious consequences.

### 3. What happens after a citizen's arrest? — CPL § 140.40

An arresting private person must transfer or attempt to transfer the arrested person into appropriate police custody **without unnecessary delay**, following the statutory procedure.

A hero cannot create a private prison, indefinitely keep a detainee, or decide what sentence the person deserves. Police may decline to take the person into custody in circumstances described by law, including where there are reasonable grounds to believe the arrest is not authorized. The civilian may need to participate in formal complaint or witness procedures.

*Marvel example:* Spider-Man restrains a robber safely, promptly summons police, provides an account of what he witnessed, and transfers the person to an officer. This fits the citizen's-arrest framework far better than taking the robber to a hidden base for questioning.

### 4. Use of force — Penal Law §§ 35.15 and 35.30

**Necessity and proportionality matter more than whether a person calls themself a hero.**

- A person can use reasonably necessary force in self-defense or defense of another when statutory conditions are met.
- A private person effecting a qualifying citizen's arrest may use reasonably necessary **nondeadly** physical force in circumstances covered by the law.
- Deadly force is subject to far narrower conditions; it is not automatically authorized whenever the underlying offense is serious.
- Once resistance or danger ends, continued violence becomes harder or impossible to justify.
- In many deadly-force self-defense situations, New York requires retreat **if the defender knows retreat can be made with complete safety**; statutory exceptions apply.
- Separate legal standards and obligations govern police officers' use of force.

**Superhuman-power implication (fictional application):** The foreseeable effect of a punch, web restraint, energy blast, or magnetic attack matters. A superhero capable of safely restraining someone may have difficulty justifying a destructive alternative. Real New York law does not supply a special formula for superhuman strength; apply the ordinary justification principles to the fictional facts.

### 5. Conduct that can expose vigilantes to criminal or civil liability

Targeting an actual criminal does **not** make every action taken against that person lawful.

| Vigilante conduct | Potential legal consequences, depending on the facts |
|---|---|
| Beating a suspect who has surrendered | Assault |
| Holding a person without legal authority | Unlawful imprisonment or, in extreme cases, kidnapping |
| Breaking into a suspect's home to gather evidence | Trespass or burglary-related liability, depending on conduct and intent |
| Destruction during a confrontation | Criminal mischief, reckless endangerment, and/or civil claims |
| Falsely presenting oneself as a police officer | Criminal impersonation |
| Physically obstructing police performing official duties | Obstructing governmental administration or other offenses |
| Threatening or injuring someone to force a confession | Coercion, menacing, assault, and potentially more serious crimes |
| Torturing a restrained suspect | Serious violent offenses |
| Killing a fleeing offender without legal justification | Potential homicide prosecution |

Relevant New York provisions can include **Penal Law §§ 135.05, 140.05, 190.25, and 195.05**, as well as assault, homicide, criminal-mischief, and other applicable statutes. Charges depend on the actual elements of an offense; the table is not an automatic one-to-one charging rule.

**Civil exposure** can include suits for injuries, damage to property, unlawful detention, and negligence even if prosecutors choose not to file criminal charges.

### 6. Masks, costumes, and secret identities

#### Historical New York anti-mask provision

During the 1999–2007 story period, **Penal Law § 240.35(4)** restricted certain public assemblies involving masked or disguised people, with statutory exceptions. It was **not** a blanket prohibition on every individual wearing a mask in public. The provision was **repealed in June 2020**.

A solitary masked hero is not therefore automatically guilty of a crime for wearing a mask. Actual activities and surrounding circumstances still matter.

#### Police questioning and identity

Under **CPL § 140.50**, police can stop a person in public and seek certain information when there is the required reasonable suspicion of specified criminal activity. A mask by itself does not establish probable cause to arrest someone. Merely declining to answer an officer's questions is not, by itself, a universal arrestable offense.

An officer might lawfully investigate a masked hero's involvement in a fight or property damage without thereby gaining unlimited authority to arrest the hero for being unidentified.

### 7. NYPD powers and restrictions

Under **CPL § 140.10**, police officers have broader arrest authority than ordinary civilians, generally based on **reasonable cause** to believe someone committed an offense, with important conditions and distinctions between crimes and lesser offenses.

Officers may also conduct lawful investigations, obtain or execute warrants, protect crime scenes, seize evidence pursuant to a lawful basis, and transport arrestees into official custody.

#### Police use of force around 2000

The NYPD Patrol Guide rules associated with **January 1, 2000** required officers to use the minimum necessary force and placed substantial restrictions on firearm use. Period guidance included restrictions against chokeholds, warning shots, shooting solely to protect property, and shooting at a fleeing felon who did not pose an imminent danger of death or serious physical injury.

A police officer does not have the right to execute criminals or use unlimited force merely because a pursuit is difficult.

**Historical-source caution:** These Patrol Guide references should be checked against an archived edition before drafting a scene that hinges on exact wording, an exception, or an officer's disciplinary exposure.

#### May police cooperate with superheroes?

**Yes, in principle.** A civilian can report crimes, provide tips and testimony, assist victims, rescue people, and cooperate with officers in lawful ways. **Penal Law § 35.30(3)** addresses situations in which a civilian is directed by police to assist in arresting or preventing escape, subject to conditions governing force.

Such cooperation does **not** automatically deputize a hero, grant independent search powers, or excuse illegal methods.

*Marvel example:* George Stacy could value Spider-Man's help during an evacuation while still requiring him to keep away from a protected crime scene or from an arrestee under investigation.

### 8. Underage heroes: D in 2000

D is **15 years old** at the March 31, 2000 continuity checkpoint. Age has legal consequences, especially for criminal procedure and custody.

- During the story's early-2000 period, New York generally treated people under **16** through its juvenile justice framework, subject to serious-offense exceptions.
- **Sixteen** was generally the historical age at which ordinary adult criminal responsibility began. New York later raised the age of criminal responsibility for most matters to 18 in stages completed in **2019**; do not project the later rule backward into 2000.
- The general citizen's-arrest statute does not establish a universal express minimum age for all such arrests, but that does not give a teenager police privileges or eliminate the risks of force, detention, or mistaken arrest.
- The children's-home staff, D's property guardian, or his financial resources do not automatically authorize vigilante conduct.

D's choices and personal actions remain author-directed; this section does **not** establish that he has become a vigilante.

### 11. Rooftop access and officially recognized superheroes — concise guide

**Rule:** Property access, police cooperation, training, and law-enforcement appointment are **four different things**. A hero can have one without the others.

| Arrangement | What it allows | What it does **not** allow |
|---|---|---|
| **Building-owner permission** | Spider-Woman may use designated parts of the Empire State Building or another privately controlled rooftop under an authorized agreement, subject to building, safety, and other applicable rules. Separate buildings generally need separate permissions. | No police powers; permission to use a roof is not permission to enter every restricted structure or occupy an unsafe spire/ledge. |
| **Ordinary civilian hero / NYPD cooperation** | Public-street patrols, rescue, defense of others, lawful citizen's arrests, reporting crimes, and voluntary communication with police. | No blanket search, seizure, interrogation, or official arrest authority. Police recognition does not confer a badge. |
| **Private security registration/training** | Employment protecting people and property within lawful contractual duties. | Graduation, licensing, or employment alone does not make the hero a police or peace officer. |
| **NYC special patrolman appointment** | Commissioner-approved **limited peace-officer** powers connected to a qualifying employer, assigned duties, geographic area, and on-duty status. | Not an automatic citywide freelance-superhero license. Commissioner supervision and restrictions apply. |
| **Sworn NYPD officer** | Full police powers after lawful hiring, appointment, and training. | Not independent vigilante authority; officer remains subject to orders, law, discipline, and procedural requirements. |
| **New superhero cooperation program** | NYC could arrange a liaison, emergency reporting channels, rescue coordination, and agreed access to certain facilities. | New categories of statutory arrest/search powers would generally require **New York State** legal authorization. |

**Practical Spider-Woman scenario:** She obtains a building manager's valid permission for a safe rooftop observation point, signs similar arrangements with other owners, and coordinates with an NYPD contact. She can still intervene in emergencies using ordinary justification and citizen's-arrest law. If she wants **official** law-enforcement powers, she needs a qualifying legal appointment, not merely completed training.

**Age and period caution:** An ordinary NYPD appointment and special-patrolman route involve eligibility requirements; teen heroes should not be assumed eligible. The sources below largely show later codifications and rules, and their **1999–2007 wording and age requirements should be verified before assigning a precise historic appointment date**. No actual Spider-Woman rooftop agreement or hero appointment is established in D's story.

**Key sources:** [NYC Administrative Code § 14-106](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/newyorkcity/latest/NYCadmin/0-0-0-24905?outputType=chromeless) (special-patrolman appointments); [NYS CPL § 2.10(27)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CPL/2.10) (peace-officer designation and limits); [NYC Rules, Title 38, Chapter 13](https://codelibrary.amlegal.com/codes/newyorkcity/latest/NYCrules/0-0-0-78094) (employer, geographic and duty restrictions).

### 12. J. Jonah Jameson calling Spider-Man a criminal — what could he legally allege?

**Bottom line:** In New York, **being a masked vigilante is not by itself a crime**. Spider-Man can generally rescue people, defend others, and make qualifying citizen's arrests with reasonably necessary force. Jameson could criticize his secrecy, judgment, and lack of oversight; those criticisms do **not** establish a criminal offense. Criminal liability depends on particular acts and proof of each offense's elements.

| Alleged Spider-Man conduct | Possible legal issue | What actually matters |
|---|---|---|
| Punching or restraining an active robber | Assault / unlawful imprisonment | Force and temporary detention may be justified by defense of others or a lawful citizen's arrest; excessive force or detention may not be. |
| Webbing a fleeing robber to a wall | Unlawful imprisonment / unreasonable force | Potentially lawful if reasonably necessary, safe, and promptly followed by transfer to police; not automatically lawful because the person is fleeing. |
| Breaking windows, damaging vehicles, or wrecking property | Criminal mischief; civil property-damage claims | Criminal charges require their own statutory elements and mental state; accident, necessity, or an emergency can alter the analysis. |
| Swinging or fighting in ways that seriously endanger bystanders | Reckless endangerment | Requires proof of the legally specified risk and culpable recklessness, not merely unusual movement or a spectacular fight. |
| Entering a closed roof or private building without permission | Trespass; in some circumstances burglary | Must assess permission, whether entry or remaining was unlawful, and—especially for burglary—intent to commit a crime inside. |
| Disrupting a lawful NYPD operation | Obstructing governmental administration | Requires conduct satisfying that offense; merely helping, disagreeing, or being present does not suffice. |
| Beating a subdued suspect | Assault or more serious offenses | Once danger or resistance has ended, justification may no longer support additional violence. |
| Wearing a mask or keeping a public secret identity | Not itself an offense | The historical group-masking rule discussed above is distinct; anonymity does not create blanket immunity from identification requirements in a specific lawful proceeding. |

**Why Jameson attacks him in Marvel stories:** Depending on the comics or adaptation, Jameson distrusts masked, unaccountable figures, questions whether Spider-Man is seeking fame, and sometimes treats him as a suspect in crimes whose full circumstances Jameson does not know. In some comics, Jameson's past experiences with masked crime and his feelings about Spider-Man's heroism inform that hostility. **These are character motives or allegations, not court findings that Spider-Man committed a crime.** Do not transfer any particular Jameson backstory or Spider-Man incident from a comic or film into D's alternate continuity unless separately adopted.

**For D's New York:** A Daily Bugle headline can fairly raise questions about needless damage, dangerous force, unauthorized entry, or interference with investigations. It cannot establish that stopping robberies or wearing a costume is illegal. George Stacy or another NYPD officer may cooperate with a law-abiding Spider-Man while investigating genuinely questionable incidents. The hero's reputation and actual legal liability are separate.

## Part II — General legal framework in D's world

**Use this part when a scene goes beyond a vigilante's street-level encounter.** The real-world law is a baseline unless D-world continuity explicitly changes it. Specific financial, scientific, character, and timeline details remain in their dedicated project masters.

### 1. Which government has authority?

| Institution or actor | Ordinary legal reach | Limit on its power |
|---|---|---|
| **U.S. federal government** | Federal crimes, constitutional rights, immigration, interstate and international matters, federal agencies, federal land, and subjects assigned by federal law | Cannot exercise unlimited general local-police authority merely by asserting a national interest |
| **New York State** | Substantive criminal law, courts, policing frameworks, property and business law, and authorization of peace officers | Subject to the U.S. and New York constitutions and applicable federal law |
| **New York City** | Local ordinances, city agencies, streets and buildings, local permits, and municipal police administration | Cannot give a civilian new state-law arrest powers solely through an informal agreement |
| **NYPD** | Policing within its jurisdiction and lawful mutual aid, with arrest, investigation, and emergency functions | Must follow statutory, constitutional, procedural, and departmental constraints |
| **Private owner / employer** | Grant property access, hire security personnel, set contractual terms, call police, and authorize specified uses of owned facilities | Cannot grant police powers, criminal immunity, or government jurisdiction |
| **Civilian / superhero** | Ordinary freedom of movement; lawful rescue, self-defense, defense of others, reporting, and citizen's arrests | Cannot use private power as a substitute for state authority or the courts |

**Federalism matters.** A state police force, local police department, federal agency, company, and international body are not automatically interchangeable. Even a national emergency is not a general legal exemption from jurisdiction, due process, and proportionality.

**D-world distinction:** S.H.I.E.L.D., Avengers arrangements, fictional alien encounters, and any superhero-specific regulation must be treated as **continuity-specific institutions or laws**. They do not automatically inherit every power of a real federal agency.

### 2. Criminal law, arrest, detention, evidence, and courts

- **Crime:** Requires the elements of a defined offense, including the required conduct and mental state. Something that looks suspicious or alarming is not automatically unlawful.
- **Detention versus arrest:** Temporarily preventing harm, a private citizen's arrest, a police investigative stop, and an official arrest are legally distinct events with different standards.
- **Reasonable force:** The legal question is not simply whether the target is a criminal; it is whether the actor's specific response is justified, necessary, and reasonable in the situation. See Part I, §§ 2–5.
- **Search and seizure:** Ordinary civilians have no general police authority to search people's homes, devices, or bodies. Officers and government agents need a lawful basis; the Fourth Amendment and applicable state law matter.
- **Evidence:** A witness can report or voluntarily provide lawfully obtained material. Breaking into a home or coercing a confession can create separate criminal or civil exposure, and may compromise a prosecution.
- **Prosecution:** Police arrest or investigate; prosecutors decide criminal charges; judges and juries adjudicate contested matters. A hero does not become judge or sentencing authority.
- **Punishment:** A civilian cannot lawfully sentence someone to confinement, pain, forfeiture, or exile. Private detention must be justified in its own right and follow the transfer rules of a citizen's arrest where relevant.

**Potential scene question:** Was the hero stopping immediate danger, making a legally authorized arrest, or punishing someone after the danger ended? Those produce different legal outcomes.

### 3. Civil law, damages, insurance, and rescue

Criminal and civil liability are separate. Even if prosecutors do not file charges, someone harmed by a hero's actions may sue for negligence, property loss, battery, false imprisonment, or another civil claim. Whether a claim succeeds depends on proof, defenses, causation, and the governing law.

| Situation | Questions to resolve |
|---|---|
| Hero pulls a pedestrian from a collapsing building | Was it a reasonable emergency response? Were injuries caused by the rescue or by the underlying disaster? Are any specific protections applicable? |
| Webbing or powers damage a car or storefront | Was there intentional or reckless destruction, negligence, necessity, or some other defense? Who actually caused the harm? |
| NovaCorp offers rescue equipment or public services | Who operates and maintains it, what representations were made, what insurance/indemnity arrangements exist, and what laws apply? |
| Private building gives a hero roof access | Who grants permission; what exact locations/hours are authorized; who addresses structural, fall, security, or insurance risks? |

**Avoid a blanket 'Good Samaritan immunity' assumption.** Specific statutory protections vary by actor, service, training, location, and type of conduct. Heroism does not categorically erase civil claims.

### 4. Property, rooftops, infrastructure, and physical access

**Owning property is not owning the law.** A landlord or owner may grant lawful access, subject to leases, easements, safety rules, and others' rights. A company that owns land or a building does not gain police powers or independence from local, state, federal, and environmental regulation.

- **Private rooftops:** Seek approval from the legally authorized owner/manager for each premises; set safe access routes and limits. The Empire State Building is privately owned, not a city-controlled public roof.
- **City buildings and streets:** City control may allow permits or access agreements, but operations still face public-safety rules and legal limits.
- **Emergency entry:** Immediate rescue or other emergency circumstances sometimes provide a legal defense or exception; they are fact-specific and do not create permanent access rights.
- **Trespass versus burglary:** Unlawful entry can be trespass; burglary adds additional criminal elements including the required intent. Mere presence on a rooftop does not automatically establish either offense.
- **Infrastructure and Alaska:** NovaCorp land, easements, permits, construction, maritime activities, and special-use sites require the relevant authorization. Alaska ownership does not establish a new sovereign state or unrestricted jurisdiction.

**Contracts:** Written agreements are often advisable, and New York's General Obligations Law § 5-701 requires a writing for specified categories of promises. Do not assume all oral permissions are invalid or all written agreements are enforceable regardless of safety or law.

### 5. Minors, guardians, education, and work (period-specific)

D's established birthday is **December 30, 1984**; he is **15 on December 30, 1999** and **18 on December 30, 2002**. The legal meaning of his age is different for contracts, employment, school, arrest procedure, criminal culpability, and guardianship. There is no single rule that being under 18 makes every activity illegal.

- **Custodial property:** A minor may own assets while a parent, appointed guardian, trustee, or statutory custodian has certain management powers and fiduciary duties. Ownership and signing authority must be distinguished.
- **Business and contracts:** A minor's authority to bind a business, enter contracts, employ others, control bank accounts, or manage trusts requires the actual applicable legal structure and representatives. Wealth does not itself make a minor legally an adult.
- **Employment and school:** Age, school attendance, work-hour limits, and industry-specific safety regulations can affect jobs and company activities. Do not substitute later rules for the 1999–2007 rules.
- **Arrest and court procedure:** Young people may be processed in distinct juvenile/offender systems under the law in effect on that date; New York's later Raise the Age changes cannot be projected backward to 2000.
- **Training and status:** Even excellent police training or a heroic track record does not waive statutory eligibility or confer official police status.

### 6. Companies, finance, employees, taxation, and regulation

NovaCorp's scope and extraordinary financial capacity **do not remove** ordinary obligations imposed on companies or individuals. The governing facts and projections remain in the designated finance master.

| Area | General rule for worldbuilding |
|---|---|
| Entity ownership | A corporation/subsidiary is a legal entity distinct from its shareholders; shares and control do not convert its assets into the owner's personal cash. |
| Contracts | Authorized signatories, consideration, legally required formalities, and applicable restrictions matter; an agreement cannot create otherwise forbidden government powers. |

## Proposed Punishments for Crimes

### Guiding Principles

1. Punishment scales with the harm done to the victim.
2. Harm that can be repaid is repaid through labor.
3. Harm that cannot be repaid is answered with hard labor for life or with death.
4. Sentences are served as labor. Idle imprisonment is not used.
5. The victim's wishes can reduce the punishment for crimes of violation (see Sexual Violation).
6. A flippant attitude makes the conditions harder at every tier. The same crime carries heavier work and harsher conditions for an offender who treats the crime or the sentence lightly.
7. Courts can view the memories of the accused and know how they feel. Facts, intent and attitude are read directly and never guessed.

---

### Memory Review

The court views the memories of the accused. This replaces witness testimony and circumstantial evidence as the basis for judgment.

- **Guilt:** The memories show what happened and who did it. A conviction rests on the accused's own memory of the act.
- **Intent:** The memories show whether the act was planned, sudden, accidental or careless. Intent sets the category of the crime.
- **Feeling:** The review reads how the accused feels about the crime and the victim. Flippancy is established by what the accused actually feels. Performed remorse and performed carelessness carry no weight.
- **Competence:** The review shows whether the accused understood the act. Offenders who could not understand it follow the Treatment Track.
- **Victim memory:** A victim's memories and feelings can be viewed with the victim's consent to measure the real extent of the damage. Without consent the damage is measured by a panel.
- **Self-defense:** The review shows whether the accused acted from genuine fear for their own life or the lives of others. Genuine self-defense or defense of others carries no punishment.

---

### Tier 1: Repayable Harm (Labor Until Repaid)

The offender works until the full debt is paid. Wages go to the victim first and then to the cost of the offender's upkeep.

| Crime | Punishment |
|---|---|
| Theft | Labor until the value of the stolen goods is repaid plus the cost of recovery |
| Property damage | Labor until the cost of repair or replacement is repaid |
| Vandalism that the people do not love (defacing or damage) | Labor until the cost of repair is repaid plus the cost of cleanup handled the same as theft |
| Vandalism that the people love | Art. No punishment |
| Fraud | The work they were trying to avoid until all money taken is repaid plus any losses the victim suffered because of it. Hard labor if flippant |
| Embezzlement | The work they were trying to avoid until the full amount is repaid plus the cost of the audit. Hard labor if flippant |
| Perjury | The offender takes the penalty the false testimony would have caused |
| Any crime that causes no harm and only involves money | Labor until the money is repaid |

**Fraud and embezzlement work:** The labor required is the work the offender was trying to avoid. An offender who is flippant about the crime or the repayment is assigned hard labor instead.

**Art exception:** Vandalism that is loved by the people is art and carries no punishment. Vandalism that is not loved is defacing or damage and is repaid the same way as theft. The public response to the work decides which category it falls in.

**Work mix:** Repayment work is a mix of general labor and skilled trade work. The trade portion gives the offender life skills and improves their recovery rate. Trade training is credited toward the debt at the same rate as labor. Skilled work earns a higher wage which speeds repayment.

**Victim choice:** The victim can choose to receive the wages or to receive the work itself. A thief who repairs the victim's house is a repayment in kind.

**Repeat offenses:** Each repeat adds a multiplier to the debt (2x for the second offense and 3x for the third).

**Refusal to work:** An offender who refuses repayment work is moved to hard labor. The debt keeps running.

---

### Tier 2: Harm to the Body or Mind (Hard Labor Scaled to Damage)

Hard labor is assigned for a term based on the extent and permanence of the damage. Medical costs and lost income are repaid on top of the term.

| Crime | Punishment |
|---|---|
| Minor assault (healed with no lasting damage) | Hard labor for a short term plus repayment of medical costs and lost wages |
| Serious assault (lasting injury) | Hard labor for a long term plus lifetime support for the victim's needs |
| Permanent disability caused | Hard labor for life |
| Harassment and stalking | Hard labor scaled to the length and severity of the harm |
| Psychological abuse and coercion | Hard labor scaled to the extent of the mental damage |
| Threats and violence without injury | Hard labor scaled to the fear and lasting distress caused |
| Kidnapping | Hard labor for a term at least equal to the time the victim was held plus scaled damage |
| Arson | Labor until the damage is repaid. Hard labor for life if anyone is hurt |

**Mental damage assessment:** The extent of the damage is measured directly from the victim's memories and feelings when the victim consents. Without consent a panel of mental health professionals rates the damage from minor to permanent. The measure sets the length of the hard labor term.

---

### Tier 3: Loss of Life

| Crime | Punishment |
|---|---|
| Murder | Hard labor for life |
| Murder of passion | The memory review of the circumstances and feeling sets the sentence |
| Killing during another crime | Hard labor for life with no chance of reduction |
| Mass murder | Hard labor for life with no chance of reduction |
| Death caused by negligence | Hard labor scaled to the degree of carelessness shown in the memories. Always below murder |

**Purpose:** The sentence makes the murderer live a life worth the weight of the life taken. It is a heavier burden than death. The murderer is forced to live on behalf of those who lost a loved one and the labor of a convicted murderer goes toward supporting the victim's dependents.

---

### Tier 4: Sexual Violation (Lifelong Harm)

These crimes cause a lifetime of pain and trauma. The punishment matches the permanence of the harm.

| Crime | Punishment |
|---|---|
| Rape | Torture followed by death |
| Rape where the victim is lenient | Death |
| Sexual abuse of a child | Torture followed by death |
| Sexual abuse of a child where the victim or guardian is lenient | Death |

**Leniency:** The victim (or the guardian of a child victim) decides whether the offender faces torture before death. The victim cannot reduce the punishment below death.

**Scope of torture:** The torture follows a mirror rule. It matches the suffering inflicted on the victim as shown in the victim's memories and never exceeds it.

---

### Summary Table

| Harm | Punishment |
|---|---|
| Money only | Labor until repaid |
| Property | Labor until repaid |
| Body (healed) | Short hard labor plus repayment |
| Body (permanent) | Long hard labor to life |
| Mind | Hard labor scaled to damage |
| Life | Hard labor for life |
| Lifelong violation | Torture then death (or death) |

---

### Seizure of Wealth

Wealthy offenders who commit crimes flippantly face a special penalty. Their wealth is seized and used to compensate the people they hurt.

- **Trigger:** A crime committed by an offender with wealth who treats the crime or the victims lightly such as relying on money to escape the consequences. The memory review establishes the attitude.
- **Seizure:** Wealth is taken in proportion to the harm done. The seizure can reach the full extent of the offender's assets.
- **Use of funds:** Seized wealth goes first to compensating the hurt. It covers repayment of the debt and medical costs. It also covers lost wages and ongoing support for victims and their dependents.
- **Labor still applies:** Seizure is added to the tier punishment. It does not replace the labor or hard labor the tier requires.
- **No buying out:** Wealth cannot be used to shorten or replace a sentence.

---

### Special Cases

- **Attempts:** An attempted crime is punished by the harm intended and the harm actually done. The memory review shows how far the offender went and why they stopped.
- **Accomplices:** Each accomplice carries a share of the debt or sentence based on their part and intent as shown in their memories.
- **Officials and public trust:** An official who commits a crime while holding power faces heavier conditions in proportion to the trust broken and the number of people harmed.
- **Institutional harm:** When an institution harms many people the debt is owed by its leaders. The memory review shows which leaders made or knew of the decision.
- **Juveniles:** Young offenders receive trade training weighted heavily and a lower ceiling for hard labor. Their case is reviewed when they reach adulthood.

#### Treatment Track

Offenders who could not understand their actions follow a treatment track. They receive treatment and supervised work. Release follows recovery as judged by a repeated memory review.

---

### Administration

- **Labor assignment:** The state assigns work based on the offender's skills first and then on need.
- **Trade placement:** Offenders in Tier 1 are placed in a rotation of general labor and skilled trades (construction, carpentry, welding, plumbing, electrical, mechanics and similar). Completed training earns a certification the offender keeps after the debt is repaid.
- **Debt tracking:** Each offender has a public ledger showing the debt owed and the amount repaid.
- **Clean slate:** Once a Tier 1 or Tier 2 debt is fully repaid the record closes. The trade certification stays with the offender.
- **Proof standard:** The memory review is the proof. Tier 3 and Tier 4 convictions require a second review by a separate panel before any sentence is carried out.
- **Wrongful conviction:** If an offender is later shown to have been wrongly convicted the state repays them through the same repayment logic. Wages are paid for all time served and the record is cleared.

---

### Crimes Against Humanity

Crimes against humanity are harms done to the public at large. Examples include PFAS contamination (3M and DuPont) and glyphosate (Roundup) as well as leaded gasoline and atrazine. The category covers poisoning or harming the public for profit or power.

**Who is guilty:** Everyone who took part. This includes those who made the decision and those who knowingly supported it or voted for it. It also includes those who covered it up. Good-faith supporters who did not know of the harm carry no punishment (see Crimes Against Humanity under Solutions). The memory review establishes who knew and who acted.

**Punishment: a living hell.**
- Indefinite hard labor with torture.
- The offender watches the world improve without them. They see the cleanup and the healing and the recovery of the people they harmed. They have no part in it and no release from it.
- There is no end date and no review that shortens the sentence.

**Wealth:** The full Seizure of Wealth section applies. All assets are taken and go to the people harmed and to the cleanup.

---

## Solutions — Additional Rules and Safeguards

### Safeguards

- **State benefit:** The state may benefit from offender labor. It funds public works and the cost of the system once victims are repaid.
- **Abuse by officials:** Any bureaucrat or official who takes advantage of the system joins the labor with torture, the same punishment as Crimes Against Humanity. This covers convicting for the sake of labor and misusing, selling or leaking memory reviews. It also covers shielding the powerful from review. The memory review applies to the reviewers and everyone who administers the system.

---

### Crimes Against Humanity

- **Threshold:** A harm is a crime against humanity when three things are shown by the memory review. The offender knew of the harm. The harm reaches a community or more. The harm continued or was left unrepaired after the knowledge existed. Anything below this line is handled as Tier 1 or Tier 2 with repayment.
- **Good faith:** Only those who knew of the harm or chose not to know are guilty. The memory review separates them from people who believed in good faith that a policy was safe. Good-faith supporters carry no punishment. Anyone who keeps supporting the policy after learning of the harm is guilty from that point.
- **Whistleblowers:** Anyone who reported or resisted or exposed the harm is exempt. Whistleblowers receive a share of the seized wealth. The memory record of the attempt establishes the exemption.
- **Past harms and estates:** The law applies to every living participant for harms that are still unrepaired. Dead offenders are traced through records and the memories of living participants. Their wealth is seized from their estates in the amount of the harm. Heirs keep nothing that came from the harm.
- **Companies:** A company that commits a crime against humanity is dissolved. Its assets go to the people harmed and to the cleanup. Its leaders are judged one by one through the memory review.

---

### Crimes and Gradations

- **Sexual offenses below rape:** These fall under Tier 2. Hard labor is scaled to the damage measured from the victim's memories and feelings. Voyeurism and sharing private images carry hard labor plus repayment of all costs the victim bears.
- **Dangerous acts without harm:** Drunk driving and reckless endangerment carry labor scaled to the risk created as read from the offender's memories. Repeats use the same multiplier as Tier 1. Any resulting harm moves the case to the tier that matches it.
- **Drug offenses:** Possession and private use carry no punishment. Treatment is available. Selling to a person who is harmed is punished by the harm done. Selling to minors is a Tier 2 offense.
- **Trust relationships:** Any Tier 2 offense against someone in the offender's care carries a term one level higher. Neglect is punished by hard labor scaled to the damage.
- **Treason and political violence:** Killing for political ends is Tier 3 with no chance of reduction. War crimes and terrorism that harm many are Crimes Against Humanity. Espionage carries hard labor scaled to the damage it caused.
- **Trafficking and slavery:** The offender serves at least the time the victims were held under the same conditions imposed on them. They also provide lifetime support for each victim. Networks that harm many are Crimes Against Humanity.
- **Multiple crimes:** An act that breaks several categories takes the highest tier. The debts and support from the lower categories are added to it. A rape that ends in death carries the Tier 4 punishment and the Tier 3 support for the victim's dependents.

### Mechanics

- **Victim consent and the mirror rule:** A victim who refuses review can choose a panel rating instead. The torture never exceeds the panel's rating of the damage. The victim can also choose death alone.
- **Guardian conflicts:** The court appoints an independent advocate to decide leniency for a child victim. The memory review of the advocate shows no conflict of interest. The child's own feelings are read and weighed.
- **Missing memories:** Intoxication is treated as self-inflicted loss of control. The memory of choosing to drink is read and treated as negligence or flippancy. Amnesia is covered by the victim's memories and other memories of the event and the physical record. Cases where nothing is recoverable go to the lower category.
- **Third-party privacy:** The review reads only memories tied to the crime. Unrelated memories are sealed and never recorded. Recordings are destroyed after the sentence is complete. Every access is logged. Any leak falls under Abuse by officials.
- **Conditions of labor:** Hard labor means heavy physical work with set hours of ten per day for six days a week. The upkeep floor guarantees food and shelter and medical care and sleep. Flippant offenders get longer hours of up to twelve and harder assignments and fewer privileges. The floor is never removed so the offender stays alive and able to work.
- **Debt valuation:** Independent assessors set the value of harm at market replacement cost. Wage rates follow the national scales for unskilled and skilled work. If a victim dies the debt goes to their dependents.
- **Age and illness:** Work is matched to ability. Options include trades and teaching and supervision and skilled remote work. Tier 4 and Tier 3 sentences apply regardless of age. Lifetime labor adjusts to what the offender can do.
- **Appeals:** Tier 1 and Tier 2 offenders get a single review by a separate panel on request. Tier 3 and Tier 4 and Crimes Against Humanity get an automatic review before any sentence is carried out.

**Real New York law for the 2003–2006 setting, subject to facts and legal process:** Penal Law § 20.00 allows criminal liability for intentional assistance in the underlying crime; a driver who intentionally helps a robbery can be liable for robbery, not merely a separate lower-level 'accessory' offense. Merely being present is insufficient. Under Penal Law § 160.15, first-degree robbery (class B violent felony) can be based on another participant's firearm or serious injury; § 160.10 defines second-degree robbery (class C violent felony). For a first violent-felony offender the ordinary prison ranges under § 70.02 are **5–25 years** for first-degree robbery and **3.5–15 years** for second-degree robbery. Third-degree robbery under § 160.05 is a class D nonviolent felony; **if actually convicted of that lesser offense**, the sentencing court may in appropriate first-offender cases impose probation, up to one year in jail, or a felony prison term capped at seven years. These alternatives are **not automatic** based on being the driver. An agreement or verdict on a lesser charge, separate analysis of assault-related charges, and any sentence have to follow the legal facts; avoid assigning Flint an invented outcome to fit a desired date. D can lawfully help fund healthcare, counsel, restitution and later employment without cancelling criminal justice process.
