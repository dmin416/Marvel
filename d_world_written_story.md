For quick reference use the established story summary; for unplayed plans and rules use the story foundation and world rules.

## September–December 1999


### Sunday, September 5, 1999: The Awakenings and Children's Home

#### The First Awakening

D woke in an unfamiliar bed.

Pale morning sunlight filtered through thin curtains into a modest bedroom. A wooden nightstand stood beside the bed, supporting an alarm clock and a glass of water. A dresser occupied one wall and a small mirror hung nearby.

D approached the mirror. His reflection was just coming into view when everything went black.

#### The Children's Home

D woke again on a noticeably less comfortable mattress. Rows of metal-framed beds stretched to either side, most occupied by sleeping boys. Tall windows admitted the morning light and a radiator knocked against the wall.

The place might have been a barracks or a prison.

A middle-aged woman wearing a blue cardigan entered with a clipboard.

"All right, boys! Up you get! Breakfast in twenty minutes!"

Several boys groaned. Beside the door, a bulletin board identified the building as a children's home. The woman addressed D and referred to his age: fourteen.

D got dressed in the clothes provided beside his bed and followed the quickest boys toward breakfast.

#### Breakfast

The dining hall was full of children eating and talking. Through the windows, taxis passed along a New York City street. A television in the corner carried a news report about Oscorp Industries and its CEO, Norman Osborn.

At the serving counter, a woman with a hairnet held a spoon over a pan of scrambled eggs.

"Morning, D. Eggs or oatmeal?"

"Could I get a little bit of each, please?"

"Sure thing, sweetheart."

She gave him some of each and added toast.

A freckled boy at a nearby table waved D over. D smiled, joined the boys and ate while listening to their conversation.

"Did you finish the math homework?" "Most of it." "Most?" "Enough."

The boys laughed. They talked about school, basketball and the weekend.

One of the boys mentioned a trip planned for Saturday, September 18, into Manhattan to help set up tables for a charity meal at F.E.A.S.T. in Chinatown. Lunch would be provided.

"You going?"

D agreed to go.

"Great!" the freckled boy said. "Mrs. Matthews said we're helping at some charity kitchen in Chinatown. F.E.A.S.T., I think it's called."

Tommy looked up from his breakfast. "You think they'll let us eat whatever's left?"

"They said we're getting lunch, Tommy."

"Yeah but what about seconds?"

The boys all laughed.

#### Cleaning Up

The other boys headed off to play. D stayed behind in the dining hall to help clean up after the meal. He stacked the plates, carried them to the gray bin and wiped down the long tables with the damp rag. The woman in the hairnet wheeled the bin into the kitchen.

#### The Flyer and the Clipboard

D read the yellow flyer on the bulletin board by the door. It asked for volunteers to set up tables for the F.E.A.S.T. community meal in Chinatown. The group would meet at the front door at 9 a.m. on Saturday, September 18 and wear closed shoes. Lunch was provided.

Mrs. Matthews' clipboard sat on a nearby table. The top sheet was a sign-up list headed "F.E.A.S.T. Saturday" with seven names and a check mark beside each one. Tommy's name and D's name were both on it. A margin note read "van seats 8 - need one more driver's helper." A second page under it held a roster of the boys' names and ages. D saw that he was signed up and had no issues.

#### The Roof

D climbed the main staircase. On the landing he straightened a crooked framed photo of the home's founders captioned "Dedicated 1921." At the top of the stairs he tried the steel door marked ROOF. It was unlocked.

The flat tar roof had a brick parapet and a rusted vent pipe. D looked at the skyline. He saw the Oscorp tower and a Stark Industries sign on a building farther down. He sat against the vent pipe.

D concluded that this was a Marvel world but that he could not tell which one. It could be anything from mild to miserable. He understood that it did not really matter. The world would survive the way it was meant to with or without him, so he should do his best and enjoy the process.

#### The Yard

D went downstairs to the yard. It was a fenced square of cracked asphalt with a basketball hoop at one end and a painted hopscotch grid at the other. Tommy and the freckled boy were playing basketball with two other boys. A smaller boy sat on the curb with a deflated ball. The freckled boy invited D to join the three on three game.

D took the smaller boy's ball and had him play on D's side for a moment. D found a hand pump in a supply closet inside the side door and inflated the ball. He watched the game from the doorway while the smaller boy scored a basket. D then gave the pumped ball to a boy by the fence who wanted one. That boy bounced it against the far wall.

D exercised at the yard's metal railing, doing pull-ups and dips. He then did push-ups and sit-ups on the asphalt and ran laps around the yard's edge. A staff member called from an upstairs window that lunch was coming soon. D slowed to a walk and cooled down by the fence.

D drank from the dented fountain by the side door. He then went to see whether the smaller boy wanted anything.

#### Lunch

The smaller boy asked D whether there was dessert at lunch because Tommy had said there was pudding on Sundays. Mrs. Matthews called the boys in for lunch. D told the smaller boy he didn't know and that it would be a surprise. D washed his hands and got in line. Mrs. Garcia, the woman in the hairnet, served him spaghetti, garlic bread and green beans.

D sat with the smaller boy at a table by the window. D asked his name. The boy said his name was Eli and that everyone knew D's name. They ate and Eli talked. He had been at the home since last spring. He liked the yard and disliked the third-floor stairs because the landing light flickered. He wanted to be a basketball player or a bus driver.

Mrs. Matthews announced a treat for good behavior. Mrs. Garcia uncovered a tray of banana pudding cups with vanilla wafers. D got in line with Eli. D gave Mrs. Garcia a hug. She was surprised, laughed, patted his back and sent him back to eat. D ate half his pudding and gave the rest to Eli. Tommy complained that Eli got seconds.

D cleared his and Eli's trays into the gray bin. Mrs. Matthews announced free time until three, then homework in the study hall. Eli asked D what he was going to do.

#### Free Time

D asked Mrs. Garcia whether she needed help. She said the two older boys had dish duty and handed him a Sunday Daily Bugle dated September 5, 1999. The front page reported that Oscorp had landed an Army contract for a "human performance program," with a photo of Norman Osborn shaking hands with a general. A smaller story covered Stark Industries unveiling a new missile line, with a photo of Tony Stark.

D kept the paper and went out to the yard with Eli. He sat in the shade by the fence and read. The inside pages carried a story about an armed crew robbing Midtown jewelers while the police had no leads, an editorial by J. Jonah Jameson and Oscorp and Stark Industries stock prices. Page six covered the F.E.A.S.T. shelter in Chinatown. It quoted founder Martin Li and said the shelter served a community meal every Saturday with help from volunteers. A photo caption named one volunteer serving soup as May Parker of Forest Hills.

D set the paper down and exercised again at the railing, then did push-ups and sit-ups and ran laps. He drank from the fountain and went inside early for study hall.

#### Study Hall

The study hall was a long ground-floor room with wooden desks, a chalkboard and bookshelves along the back wall. Mr. Reyes, a thin young staff member with rolled shirtsleeves, told D to take his folder from the cubbies by the door. D looked over the bookshelves first. They held a set of 1994 World Book encyclopedias, dictionaries, an atlas, donated paperbacks and a stack of old Popular Science magazines.

D took the green folder from his cubby and sat in the second row. It held a Midtown High schedule with his name and homeroom, a math worksheet on linear equations and a page of English vocabulary, both due Tuesday, September 14, when D was scheduled to begin at Midtown High. D finished both quickly. He then brought the stack of Popular Science magazines to his desk.

D read the Popular Science issues from 1998 and 1999. One cover asked whether powered armor was the future of the battlefield and quoted a Stark Industries engineer who called it decades away. A short piece covered Dr. Curt Connors of Empire State University and his work on limb regeneration in reptiles. Connors' right sleeve was pinned up at the elbow and his funding had been cut twice. A six-page profile covered Oscorp's new research campus, its move into military biotechnology, research head Dr. Mendel Stromm and Oscorp's approaches to university labs, including Empire State's. A business item profiled Victor Von Doom, a younger son of Latveria's royal family who chose business abroad and founded Von Doom Industries in New York. His company had bought three aerospace suppliers and was rumored to be planning a private orbital platform. A mail-order page listed hobby kits, with a robot arm kit circled and "someday" written beside it.

Eli could not understand his math sheet. D asked what confused him. Eli did not understand how a letter could be a number. D walked him through 3x + 5 = 20, explaining the equation as a balance and checking the answer by putting it back in. Eli solved the next problem alone and D checked it. When Eli had one wrong, D told him to check every answer the same way. Eli found and fixed the mistake himself.

Mr. Reyes asked whether D tutored and said a few boys could use the help. D told him he would be glad to help other boys. Quiet work ended at 4:30.

#### Evening Exercise

D returned the Popular Science magazines to the back shelf and put his folder in his cubby. He went out to the yard and Eli followed to watch from the curb by the fountain. D did pull-ups and dips at the railing, push-ups and sit-ups on the asphalt and laps around the fence line. Eli ran half a lap beside him before dropping back to the curb. D finished at 5:20 with dinner at six.

#### Sunday Evening

Dinner was at six in the same hall: baked chicken, rice, canned green beans and bread. Chores followed, with the dishes going to the two older boys. The common room had one television and a staff member put the evening news on it. The lead story was the Midtown jewelry robbery. Showers ran in shifts down the hall from the dormitory. Lights out was at nine.

The building showed its size over those hours. Three or four staff covered about sixty boys. One woman ran the kitchen. Hand-me-down clothes were sorted by size in a closet off the dormitory. A pay phone stood in the front hall under a list of rules. The office by it stayed locked with a filing cabinet visible behind the glass. Mrs. Matthews read the Saturday F.E.A.S.T. list aloud again at dinner and reminded the seven boys about closed shoes. She also reminded the boys about the upcoming school term. D's Midtown High attendance was scheduled to begin Tuesday, September 14, with the school bus leaving the front door at 7:15 a.m.

### September 6–12, 1999: No recorded scenes

No scenes from these days have been played or recorded here. Labor Day fell on Monday, September 6 and New York City public schools opened Thursday, September 9. D's first attendance at Midtown High remains September 14. The planned F.E.A.S.T. outing remains Saturday, September 18. No additional actions or encounters are assumed for this interval.

### Monday, September 13, 1999: Home and enrollment

D spent the day at the home with chores and the study table. Study hall ran from four to five-thirty. Two boys a year behind D were already waiting at the second row, Marcus with the same linear equations sheet Eli had worked on and Benny with fractions. D ran the balance idea again with Marcus, writing each step out and making him check the answer by putting it back in. Benny had never been told that a fraction was a division, so D wrote it as a bar and then as a divide sign and let him do the same problem twice. Eli came over partway through and sat in on it. Mr. Reyes came by twice and said he would rather have four boys finishing their sheets than one. He then asked whether D would take the same table on school nights. D told him yes and Mr. Reyes wrote it on the back of his own folder.

D trained in the yard in the last hour of light: pull-ups and dips at the railing, push-ups and sit-ups on the asphalt and laps along the fence. Eli sat on the curb by the fountain with the pumped-up ball. Marcus tried the railing, got three pull-ups and ran one lap.

Dinner was sausage and peppers over rice, bread and canned pears. Mrs. Matthews read the Saturday list aloud again. Chores, the common room, showers in shifts and lights out at nine followed.

### Tuesday, September 14, 1999: First day at Midtown

The bus crossed the bridge into Queens. Midtown High School of Science and Technology stood behind a chain-link fence and a parking lot, four floors of brick with a banner over the doors for the fall term.

Homeroom took attendance twice before the teacher got the list right. The boy at the next desk introduced himself and asked where D came from, which lunch period he had and whether he knew anybody. D received textbooks with other students' names inked in the covers, syllabi and a form for a parent to sign. D worked through everything the teachers put in front of him, read ahead in the chapters while the rooms were still settling and copied out the whole year's unit list from the algebra syllabus. In biology the terrariums along the window were labeled in a careful hand and the first lab was two weeks out. The English teacher wanted a notebook kept daily.

Lunch was turkey and gravy over bread, corn, an apple and milk. A few students made room at a table and talked through it, mostly about teachers and who was tough. The food was plain. D ate all of it and went back for a second apple.

In his last period a girl asked to share a book because hers had not come in. She spent the period pointing out which pages the teacher would skip. She said the library stayed open until four and nobody ever used the back tables.

Last bell was at 2:45 and the bus left at 3:00. D took the bus. The ride back crossed the bridge with the river murky underneath and the skyline off to the left. The bus let the boys out at the front door a little before four. Mrs. Matthews checked them in and collected the parent forms, which she signed herself at the desk.

Study hall at four sent three boys to the second-row table, two of them from the day before. D trained in the yard before dinner. Dinner was at six. The evening news carried an arrest in the Midtown jewelry case and said two of the crew were still out, with some of the jewelry recovered and a lot of it not. Lights out at nine.

### Wednesday to Friday, September 15 to 17, 1999

Wednesday ran full periods. Algebra continued its first chapter at a pace built for the slowest student in the room and the homework came back graded the next morning. Biology spent the hour on microscopes, two students to a scope, with prepared slides from a box that had been in the cabinet a long time and handed out the lab safety sheet to be signed. English collected the first notebook entry. The history teacher talked for the whole period without notes. The hallway sorted itself out more clearly than on D's first day: which stairwell to use and which hall was impassable at the bell. A couple of students talked to D at the lockers, one comparing the algebra homework and another asking whether he had a lunch period free for a study group that met in the cafeteria corner. Lunch was fish sticks, tater tots, green beans, a roll and milk. D ate all of it. By the end of the day D had worked out the stairwells.

Thursday the teachers started separating the class out. The algebra teacher put a harder problem set on the board for anyone who wanted it and said the school's math team met in the spring. The biology teacher asked who wanted the terrarium feeding duty, a Friday job that came with a key to the room's supply cabinet. The English notebook came back with marks on it. Someone from the cafeteria corner study group asked D whether he was coming, since the first real test in any class would land the next week.

Friday brought a quiz in algebra, the first vocabulary test in English and the signed lab sheets collected. The fall club sign-ups went up outside the office with deadlines on them. The weight room was open after school with its sheet already half full of older boys' names. The last bell came early for a pep rally in the gym that the freshmen were herded into, with the fall teams brought out and the big kid from the hallway on the floor with them.

At the home the evenings ran the same each night: the bus back, study hall from four to five-thirty with whoever Mr. Reyes sent, some time in the yard before dinner, dinner at six, chores, the common room and lights out at nine. The number at the second-row table went from two boys to four by Friday. Mrs. Matthews read the F.E.A.S.T. list aloud at dinner twice more and on Friday night added that the van would leave at nine sharp and would not wait.

The news that week carried the two jewelry crew members still loose, the storm off the coast turning out to sea and an Oscorp spokesman on Thursday saying the Army contract would create three hundred jobs in Queens.

### Saturday, September 18, 1999: F.E.A.S.T.

Seven boys from the home rode the van into Chinatown with Mr. Reyes driving. D took the driver's helper seat with the map and pointed out a parking spot on Mott Street. D carried the bag of rolls into F.E.A.S.T., where he met May Parker, the volunteer from the newspaper photo. He carried trays out to the tables and took a spot on the serving line as the doors opened at 10:00.

A boy with glasses reached the front of the line. D filled his plate and told him to enjoy it and May said the boy was her nephew Peter. D filled a plate for himself, asked Peter whether the seat beside him was taken and sat down at the end of a table. He introduced himself. Peter was a freshman at Midtown High who built radios and small circuits from flea market parts, was working on a remote-controlled car and photographed bridges and rooftops with an old Pentax from his uncle Ben. D told Peter he went to the same school, though they did not share class times. D told Peter about the children's home. Peter ate lunch in the science wing on Tuesdays and Thursdays and D told him he would see him there Tuesday. Peter said he would bring the radio he was building.

May told D that Ben wanted to meet him and that Sunday dinner in Queens was open if the home allowed it. D thanked May for the meal. When Mr. Reyes said he would take the dinner to the home's director on Monday and that May would call the home with details if the director approved, D told them he would help out if they asked. May answered that Ben could always use someone to carry extra chairs up from the basement.

D helped clear tables with the other boys from the home and carried the last stack of dishes to the kitchen with Peter. D said goodbye to Peter at the coat rack and waved to him across the hall, said goodbye to May and lined up with the boys at the doors. D rode home in the front seat of the van and gave Mr. Reyes directions, including around a double-parked truck. Mr. Reyes parked in a spot D pointed out near the home's front steps. D held the side door while the younger boys climbed down and followed them up the steps into the entry hall. A volunteer at the front desk looked up from her papers as he crossed the floor. Eli came in through the side door from the yard with the ball and asked D how F.E.A.S.T. went.

### Sunday to Friday, September 19 to 24, 1999

Saturday afternoon and evening followed the home's usual routine and Sunday the same. Monday was Yom Kippur and Midtown High stayed closed. Mr. Reyes took May's Sunday dinner request to the director that morning and ran the study table in the afternoon for Marcus, Benny, Eli and two more boys.

Tuesday D rode the bus to Midtown High and ate lunch in the science wing with Peter. Peter showed him the radio he was building, with a coil he had wound and a dial he had salvaged from a flea market. The only other person in the room was a girl named Gwen, who sat at the far bench with a notebook and a physics problem set from an older student's textbook. Peter introduced them. She nodded and returned to the page. D asked her what the problem was about and she explained the first step in one sentence. He asked a follow-up question about the next step and she answered it in three.

Wednesday ran regular classes and the cafeteria-corner study group, where D worked through the algebra homework with two students from his period. Study hall at four had five boys at the table. Thursday's lunch in the science wing picked up where Tuesday's had left off. D sat at the next bench with his algebra book. Gwen checked a calculation he had written on scratch paper and told him a sign was wrong in the third line. D redid it and the answer matched hers. She asked him what he was reading for English and Peter joined in with the radio.

Friday the quiz grades came back and the weekend schedule went up at the home. At dinner Mrs. Matthews announced that May Parker had called the home that afternoon and that the director had approved Sunday dinner in Queens, day only, with Mr. Reyes driving. Mrs. Matthews also discussed future F.E.A.S.T. scheduling, without establishing another outing for September 25.

### Saturday, September 25 to Wednesday, September 29, 1999

Saturday was a regular day at the home. The van stayed parked, D trained in the yard and the tutoring table ran at four with six boys.

Sunday Mr. Reyes drove D to Forest Hills. Ben met him at the door of the house and shook his hand. May had dinner on the table and Ben asked D about the home and about school. D carried the extra folding chairs up from the basement and set them around the table. Partway through the meal, Mary Jane Watson's father began shouting at her from the porch next door. May went over, brought Mary Jane back to the Parkers' table and set a plate for her. Peter pulled out a chair beside his and gave her half of his dessert. May introduced D to her as Peter's new friend. Mr. Reyes collected D at the agreed hour and drove him back to the home.

Monday was a regular school day with study hall at four. Tuesday D ate lunch in the science wing with Peter and Gwen. Gwen asked D what he thought of the biology chapter and he gave her his answer and added a question about the terrarium labs. She answered it and asked him another.

Wednesday the first tests of the year were handed back in algebra and biology. At lunch the gym was set up for the club fair, with tables for the Science Club, Robotics, Photography, the Newspaper and Academic Decathlon. D walked the tables with Peter and they stopped at the Photography table, where Peter set the Pentax down to fill out a sign-up sheet. Flash Thompson, shorter than the friends walking with him, came over loud and relaxed. He asked Peter to take his picture for the yearbook and posed in front of the Photography poster. He joked that Peter's Pentax was older than both of them and his friends laughed. Peter answered with a joke of his own and nothing was touched or taken. Flash shook D's hand and introduced himself as the fastest freshman in the school, then went back to the football table with his friends.

#### Night: The rooftop, the wish and the meteor

- On **his first birthday in this world**, D is on the roof. He sees what appears to be a **shooting star** and wishes for **an interesting future**.
- The object comes down onto the roof and **crushes an air-conditioning vent**. Despite the heat, its approach is strangely **slow**, like the symbiote meteor in *Spider-Man 3*. Damage is local to the vent.
- A dark, living **symbiote crawls out** and moves toward him. It stirs a faint **sense of familiarity** in his sealed memory.
- Curious, D **pokes the symbiote**. It immediately tries to **envelop and possess him**.
- **The seal cracks.** The attempted takeover forces a **surface-level crack** in the seal over D's memories. The archive behind it has always held **one trillion lifetimes** but the crack exposes only its flat, surface layer: a two-dimensional "wall" of **one million lifetimes**. Even that surface is far more than the symbiote can process. **The symbiote, not D, convulses and loses control as its mind is overwhelmed and destroyed.**
- D's mind adapts to the capacity. She looked back at him, pleased. “I'd like that too.”

“One minute!” somebody called.

People cheered and started checking their watches. Children bounced excitedly beside their families. Lorna looked from the crowd to D, then slipped a hand out of her pocket and reached for his.

“Ten! Nine! Eight!”

Cheers rose around them as fireworks broke across the sky. Gold, blue, red, and silver light flashed above the Unisphere. Lorna looked up, delighted.

“Happy New Year, D!”

“Happy New Year, Lorna!”

She hesitated only a moment before stepping forward and wrapping her arms around him. D returned the hug immediately.

It was their second hug, after the one they'd shared on his birthday. This one lasted a little longer than either expected. Lorna felt happy to be there with someone she enjoyed and was learning to trust. She didn't need to turn that feeling into a declaration; being close to him was enough.

They let the hug end, but Lorna reached for his hand once more. Another burst of fireworks opened overhead, bright silver and gold.

They watched the display, commenting on the colors and laughing when an especially loud explosion startled people nearby. After a while, Lorna leaned lightly against his shoulder. D stayed beside her, their fingers comfortably intertwined.

Neither had said anything about dating. They were friends getting closer, beginning to recognize the possibility of something more. Lorna didn't need to know exactly where those feelings would lead to enjoy them now.

“Yeah, Lorna?”

He smiled. “I'd like that.”

She squeezed his hand once more. When it was time to leave, they returned to their respective supervised groups, taking the warmth of that evening with them into the new year.

## Research note — Cindy Moon's three-source superspider (October 17, 2002)

*Fictional D-world research design, using traits of spiders known by 2002. The project's fifteen specimens are genetically engineered spiders on display, **not fifteen source species**. Columbia's real-world possession of these specimens is not asserted.*

**Primary research objective:** engineer silk that is much stronger, tougher, more durable, and more versatile than conventional spider silk. The other traits support the spider's movement and performance, rather than expanding the experiment into a many-species superorganism.

| Source spider or group | Intended contribution | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| **Golden orb-weaver** (*Nephila* species, e.g. *N. clavipes*) | Strong, tough, elastic silk and improved silk production | **Primary** |
| **Jumping spider** (Salticidae) | Acute eyesight, precise jumping, coordination, rapid responses | Supporting |
| **Huntsman spider** (Sparassidae) | Speed, agility, quick multidirectional movement across surfaces | Supporting |

**Only these three source groups contribute to the engineered spider design.** Crab spiders, fishing spiders, and other suggested groups are **not** part of this experiment. The broader natural-history catalog is not a list of genetic donors.

**Venom and bite continuity:** The research team did **not** select for venom or design the superspider to be dangerous to people. That does not prove a bite is safe: many spiders have venom, and the combined fictional genetic modifications could produce unforeseen effects. Gwen's initial estimate that the bite is unlikely to be fatal is her reasonable but incomplete assessment. The same spider bites both Gwen and D; D’s symbiote intercepts the introduced material. His spider abilities emerge alongside Gwen’s over the next several days, while the symbiote continually integrates and optimizes the adaptations across his body; mastery still requires time.

**Historical framing:** Golden orb-weaver silk properties, jumping spider visual behavior, and huntsman agility were known before October 2002. The ability to combine them into a superspider with human-transforming effects is fictional Marvel science, not a documented Columbia University program.

### Monday, December 30, 2002 — A Place of Their Own
