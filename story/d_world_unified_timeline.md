## Proposed first real disagreement: “Are you always this nice?”

**Status: unplayed, optional scene outline.** Best placement: a quiet evening after several months dating, perhaps late June, July or September. Their May 13 mutual declaration of love is real and remains intact. This moment introduces a *new* imperfection in their understanding, not a retroactive cancellation of trust.

**Trigger:** D repeatedly says “whatever you want” when Lorna asks where he'd like to go, or politely agrees in ways that make his own preferences hard to see. He thinks he's being considerate. Lorna hears rehearsed sweetness. She worries he is editing himself to keep her happy and will eventually resent it—or that he doesn't trust her enough to be candid.

**The argument (sample exchange; adjust during play):**

> “You don't have to agree with everything I say,” Lorna tells him. “I want to know what *you* think.”
>
> “I do tell you what I think.”
>
> “Not always. Sometimes it sounds like you're saying what you think I want to hear.”
>
> D looks genuinely taken aback. “I thought I was giving you a choice.”
>
> “I know. But it doesn't feel like a choice if you hide yours.”
>
> “That's fair,” he says, after a moment. “I wanted to go to the planetarium, actually. I should have said so.”
>
> “Then say that. I like you, D. I don't need you to be perfect.”
>
> “I'm not perfect. And I'm not being nice to make you owe me anything. I love you, and I want to treat you well. That's how I try to treat people. But I can tell you when I want something different.”
>
> Lorna is quiet for a moment. “It's hard for me to believe someone can really mean that.”
>
> “You don't have to believe it all at once. Just let me keep being myself—and tell me when I'm not being clear.”

**What proves it without contrivance:** D does not deliver a grand speech or win her over with gifts. He demonstrates his actual character by naming his own preference, keeping a reasonable boundary when they genuinely disagree later, being equally courteous to people from whom he expects nothing, and making no demand for reassurance or praise afterward. Lorna notices a consistent pattern over days or weeks. When they discuss it again, she acknowledges she mistook his natural kindness for performance; he acknowledges that excessive deference can feel like not showing up honestly. **Both apologize where warranted.** They remain affectionate, and the disagreement becomes a way to understand each other more accurately.

**Important character balance:** Lorna's disbelief is not that no kind human could exist at all; she has difficulty trusting that this particular boy's care is steady, sincere and offered without hidden conditions, especially given her earlier disappointments. That doubt is believable, not proof she is foolish. D's kindness is authentic, but he has agency, likes and dislikes, and can say no. Avoid portraying either teenager as a saint or as emotionally broken. Do not resolve Lorna's deepest abandonment concerns in a single evening: reserve more intense vulnerability and future discussions about her father for later, where earned.
