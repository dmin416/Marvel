D Story: Story So Far

Third-person, past-tense interactive narrative. Only events already played are recorded. Later days are recorded at the level they were played, which for the school days is a summary rather than scene by scene. This file is the only story canon; everything in the other D story files is planning ideas.

The First Awakening

D woke in an unfamiliar bed.

Pale morning sunlight filtered through thin curtains into a modest bedroom. A wooden nightstand stood beside the bed, supporting an alarm clock and a glass of water. A dresser occupied one wall and a small mirror hung nearby.

D approached the mirror. His reflection was just coming into view when everything went black.

The Children's Home

D woke again on a noticeably less comfortable mattress. Rows of metal-framed beds stretched to either side, most occupied by sleeping boys. Tall windows admitted the morning light and a radiator knocked against the wall.

The place might have been a barracks or a prison.

A middle-aged woman wearing a blue cardigan entered with a clipboard.

"All right, boys! Up you get! Breakfast in twenty minutes!"

Several boys groaned. Beside the door, a bulletin board identified the building as a children's home. The woman addressed D and referred to his age: fourteen.

D got dressed in the clothes provided beside his bed and followed the quickest boys toward breakfast.

Breakfast

The dining hall was full of children eating and talking. Through the windows, taxis passed along a New York City street. A television in the corner carried a news report about Oscorp Industries and its CEO, Norman Osborn.

At the serving counter, a woman with a hairnet held a spoon over a pan of scrambled eggs.

"Morning, D. Eggs or oatmeal?"

"Could I get a little bit of each, please?"

"Sure thing, sweetheart."

She gave him some of each and added toast.

A freckled boy at a nearby table waved D over. D smiled, joined the boys and ate while listening to their conversation.

"Did you finish the math homework?"
"Most of it."
"Most?"
"Enough."

The boys laughed. They talked about school, basketball and the weekend.

One of the boys mentioned a Saturday trip into Manhattan to help set up tables for a charity meal at F.E.A.S.T. in Chinatown. Lunch would be provided.

"You going?"

D agreed to go.

"Great!" the freckled boy said. "Mrs. Matthews said we're helping at some charity kitchen in Chinatown. F.E.A.S.T., I think it's called."

Tommy looked up from his breakfast. "You think they'll let us eat whatever's left?"

"They said we're getting lunch, Tommy."

"Yeah but what about seconds?"

The boys all laughed.

Cleaning Up

The other boys headed off to play. D stayed behind in the dining hall to help clean up after the meal. He stacked the plates, carried them to the gray bin and wiped down the long tables with the damp rag. The woman in the hairnet wheeled the bin into the kitchen.

The Flyer and the Clipboard

D read the yellow flyer on the bulletin board by the door. It asked for volunteers to set up tables for the F.E.A.S.T. community meal in Chinatown. The group would meet at the front door at 9 a.m. on Saturday and wear closed shoes. Lunch was provided.

Mrs. Matthews' clipboard sat on a nearby table. The top sheet was a sign-up list headed "F.E.A.S.T. Saturday" with seven names and a check mark beside each one. Tommy's name and D's name were both on it. A margin note read "van seats 8 - need one more driver's helper." A second page under it held a roster of the boys' names and ages. D saw that he was signed up and had no issues.

The Roof

D climbed the main staircase. On the landing he straightened a crooked framed photo of the home's founders captioned "Dedicated 1921." At the top of the stairs he tried the steel door marked ROOF. It was unlocked.

The flat tar roof had a brick parapet and a rusted vent pipe. D looked at the skyline. He saw the Oscorp tower and a Stark Industries sign on a building farther down. He sat against the vent pipe.

D concluded that this was a Marvel world but that he could not tell which one. It could be anything from mild to miserable. He understood that it did not really matter. The world would survive the way it was meant to with or without him, so he should do his best and enjoy the process.

The Yard

D went downstairs to the yard. It was a fenced square of cracked asphalt with a basketball hoop at one end and a painted hopscotch grid at the other. Tommy and the freckled boy were playing basketball with two other boys. A smaller boy sat on the curb with a deflated ball. The freckled boy invited D to join the three on three game.

D took the smaller boy's ball and had him play on D's side for a moment. D found a hand pump in a supply closet inside the side door and inflated the ball. He watched the game from the doorway while the smaller boy scored a basket. D then gave the pumped ball to a boy by the fence who wanted one. That boy bounced it against the far wall.

D exercised at the yard's metal railing, doing pull-ups and dips. He then did push-ups and sit-ups on the asphalt and ran laps around the yard's edge. A staff member called from an upstairs window that lunch was coming soon. D slowed to a walk and cooled down by the fence.

D drank from the dented fountain by the side door. He then went to see whether the smaller boy wanted anything.

Lunch

The smaller boy asked D whether there was dessert at lunch because Tommy had said there was pudding on Saturdays. Mrs. Matthews called the boys in for lunch. D told the smaller boy he didn't know and that it would be a surprise. D washed his hands and got in line. Mrs. Garcia, the woman in the hairnet, served him spaghetti, garlic bread and green beans.

D sat with the smaller boy at a table by the window. D asked his name. The boy said his name was Eli and that everyone knew D's name. They ate and Eli talked. He had been at the home since last spring. He liked the yard and disliked the third-floor stairs because the landing light flickered. He wanted to be a basketball player or a bus driver.

Mrs. Matthews announced a treat for good behavior. Mrs. Garcia uncovered a tray of banana pudding cups with vanilla wafers. D got in line with Eli. D gave Mrs. Garcia a hug. She was surprised, laughed, patted his back and sent him back to eat. D ate half his pudding and gave the rest to Eli. Tommy complained that Eli got seconds.

D cleared his and Eli's trays into the gray bin. Mrs. Matthews announced free time until three, then homework in the study hall. Eli asked D what he was going to do.

Free Time

D asked Mrs. Garcia whether she needed help. She said the two older boys had dish duty and handed him a Sunday Daily Bugle dated September 12, 1999. The front page reported that Oscorp had landed an Army contract for a "human performance program," with a photo of Norman Osborn shaking hands with a general. A smaller story covered Stark Industries unveiling a new missile line, with a photo of Tony Stark.

D kept the paper and went out to the yard with Eli. He sat in the shade by the fence and read. The inside pages carried a story about an armed crew robbing Midtown jewelers while the police had no leads, an editorial by J. Jonah Jameson and Oscorp and Stark Industries stock prices. Page six covered the F.E.A.S.T. shelter in Chinatown. It quoted founder Martin Li and said the shelter served a community meal every Saturday with help from volunteers. A photo caption named one volunteer serving soup as May Parker of Forest Hills.

D set the paper down and exercised again at the railing, then did push-ups and sit-ups and ran laps. He drank from the fountain and went inside early for study hall.

Study Hall

The study hall was a long ground-floor room with wooden desks, a chalkboard and bookshelves along the back wall. Mr. Reyes, a thin young staff member with rolled shirtsleeves, told D to take his folder from the cubbies by the door. D looked over the bookshelves first. They held a set of 1994 World Book encyclopedias, dictionaries, an atlas, donated paperbacks and a stack of old Popular Science magazines.

D took the green folder from his cubby and sat in the second row. It held a Midtown High schedule with his name and homeroom, a math worksheet on linear equations and a page of English vocabulary, both due Monday. D finished both quickly. He then brought the stack of Popular Science magazines to his desk.

D read the Popular Science issues from 1998 and 1999. One cover asked whether powered armor was the future of the battlefield and quoted a Stark Industries engineer who called it decades away. A short piece covered Dr. Curt Connors of Empire State University and his work on limb regeneration in reptiles. Connors' right sleeve was pinned up at the elbow and his funding had been cut twice. A six-page profile covered Oscorp's new research campus, its move into military biotechnology, research head Dr. Mendel Stromm and Oscorp's approaches to university labs, including Empire State's. A business item profiled Victor Von Doom, a younger son of Latveria's royal family who chose business abroad and founded Von Doom Industries in New York. His company had bought three aerospace suppliers and was rumored to be planning a private orbital platform. A mail-order page listed hobby kits, with a robot arm kit circled and "someday" written beside it.

Eli could not understand his math sheet. D asked what confused him. Eli did not understand how a letter could be a number. D walked him through 3x + 5 = 20, explaining the equation as a balance and checking the answer by putting it back in. Eli solved the next problem alone and D checked it. When Eli had one wrong, D told him to check every answer the same way. Eli found and fixed the mistake himself.

Mr. Reyes asked whether D tutored and said a few boys could use the help. D told him he would be glad to help other boys. Quiet work ended at 4:30.

Evening Exercise

D returned the Popular Science magazines to the back shelf and put his folder in his cubby. He went out to the yard and Eli followed to watch from the curb by the fountain. D did pull-ups and dips at the railing, push-ups and sit-ups on the asphalt and laps around the fence line. Eli ran half a lap beside him before dropping back to the curb. D finished at 5:20 with dinner at six.

Sunday Evening

Dinner was at six in the same hall: baked chicken, rice, canned green beans and bread. Chores followed, with the dishes going to the two older boys. The common room had one television and a staff member put the evening news on it. The lead story was the Midtown jewelry robbery. Showers ran in shifts down the hall from the dormitory. Lights out was at nine.

The building showed its size over those hours. Three or four staff covered about sixty boys. One woman ran the kitchen. Hand-me-down clothes were sorted by size in a closet off the dormitory. A pay phone stood in the front hall under a list of rules. The office by it stayed locked with a filing cabinet visible behind the glass. Mrs. Matthews read the Saturday F.E.A.S.T. list aloud again at dinner and reminded the seven boys about closed shoes. She also gave the morning's schedule: breakfast at 6:45 and the Midtown High bus at 7:15 from the front door.

Monday, September 13, 1999: First Day at Midtown High

The bus crossed the bridge into Queens. Midtown High School of Science and Technology stood behind a chain-link fence and a parking lot, four floors of brick with a banner over the doors for the fall term.

Homeroom took attendance twice before the teacher got the list right. The boy at the next desk introduced himself and asked where D came from, which lunch period he had and whether he knew anybody. The first-day classes ran short periods and handed out textbooks with other students' names inked in the covers, a syllabus each and a form for a parent to sign. D worked through everything the teachers put in front of him, read ahead in the chapters while the rooms were still settling and copied out the whole year's unit list from the algebra syllabus. In biology the terrariums along the window were labeled in a careful hand and the first lab was two weeks out. The English teacher wanted a notebook kept daily.

Lunch was turkey and gravy over bread, corn, an apple and milk. A few students made room at a table and talked through it, mostly about teachers and who was tough. The food was plain. D ate all of it and went back for a second apple.

In his last period a girl asked to share a book because hers had not come in. She spent the period pointing out which pages the teacher would skip. She said the library stayed open until four and nobody ever used the back tables.

Last bell was at 2:45 and the bus left at 3:00. D took the bus. The ride back crossed the bridge with the river flat and brown underneath and the skyline off to the left. The bus let the boys out at the front door a little before four. Mrs. Matthews checked them in and collected the parent forms, which she signed herself at the desk.

Monday Study Hall and Evening

Study hall ran from four to five-thirty on school nights. Two boys a year behind D were already waiting at the second row, Marcus with the same linear equations sheet Eli had worked on and Benny with fractions. D ran the balance idea again with Marcus, writing each step out and making him check the answer by putting it back in. Benny had never been told that a fraction was a division, so D wrote it as a bar and then as a divide sign and let him do the same problem twice. Eli came over partway through and sat in on it. Mr. Reyes came by twice and said he would rather have four boys finishing their sheets than one. He then asked whether D would take the same table on school nights. D told him yes and Mr. Reyes wrote it on the back of his own folder.

D trained in the yard in the last hour of light: pull-ups and dips at the railing, push-ups and sit-ups on the asphalt and laps along the fence. Eli sat on the curb by the fountain with the pumped-up ball. Marcus tried the railing, got three pull-ups and ran one lap.

Dinner was sausage and peppers over rice, bread and canned pears. Mrs. Matthews read the Saturday list aloud again. Chores, the common room, showers in shifts and lights out at nine followed.

Tuesday, September 14, 1999

Tuesday ran full periods instead of Monday's short ones. Algebra started the first chapter. Biology spent the hour on microscopes, two students to a scope, with prepared slides from a box that had been in the cabinet a long time. English collected the first notebook entry. The history teacher talked for the whole period without notes.

The hallway sorted itself out more clearly than on Monday: which stairwell to use and which hall was impassable at the bell. A couple of students talked to D at the lockers, one comparing the algebra homework and another asking whether he had a lunch period free for a study group that met in the cafeteria corner.

Lunch was fish sticks, tater tots, green beans, a roll and milk. D ate all of it.

After school came the bus back and study hall at four, where Mr. Reyes sent three boys to the table, two of them from the day before. D trained in the yard until the light went. Dinner was at six. The evening news carried an arrest in the Midtown jewelry case and said two of the crew were still out, with some of the jewelry recovered and a lot of it not. Lights out at nine.

Wednesday to Friday, September 15 to 17, 1999

Wednesday the week settled into its real shape. Algebra moved through the first chapter at a pace built for the slowest student in the room and the homework came back graded the next morning. Biology handed out the lab safety sheet to be signed and read the first chapter aloud. The history teacher went another full period without notes. In the hallway the freshmen had worked out the stairwells by now.

Thursday the teachers started separating the class out. The algebra teacher put a harder problem set on the board for anyone who wanted it and said the school's math team met in the spring. The biology teacher asked who wanted the terrarium feeding duty, a Friday job that came with a key to the room's supply cabinet. The English notebook came back with marks on it. Someone from the cafeteria corner study group asked D whether he was coming, since the first real test in any class would land the next week.

Friday brought a quiz in algebra, the first vocabulary test in English and the signed lab sheets collected. The fall club sign-ups went up outside the office with deadlines on them. The weight room was open after school with its sheet already half full of older boys' names. The last bell came early for a pep rally in the gym that the freshmen were herded into, with the fall teams brought out and the big kid from the hallway on the floor with them.

At the home the evenings ran the same each night: the bus back, study hall from four to five-thirty with whoever Mr. Reyes sent, the yard until the light went, dinner at six, chores, the common room and lights out at nine. The number at the second-row table went from two boys to four by Friday. Mrs. Matthews read the F.E.A.S.T. list aloud at dinner twice more and on Friday night added that the van would leave at nine sharp and would not wait.

The news that week carried the two jewelry crew members still loose, the storm off the coast turning out to sea and an Oscorp spokesman on Thursday saying the Army contract would create three hundred jobs in Queens.
